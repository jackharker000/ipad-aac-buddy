01The shape of the whole thing

Parley is a turn-triggered system, not a polling one. Nothing happens on a timer. The Silero VAD decides that somebody stopped talking, and that single event fans out into everything else: embed the voice, transcribe the words, work out who it was, assemble a prompt, stream six replies onto the screen. When James taps one, it goes to TTS in his cloned voice.

Three properties shape every decision below. Latency — a reply that lands in three seconds is a reply James won't use, so anything that can run in parallel does, and anything that can be stale by one turn is. Speaker-ID accuracy — the prompt is only as good as its idea of who is in the room. And key safety — the iPad never holds an API key; every provider call goes through a thin /api/* route.

02The live turn loop

This is the hot path, and it is the reason the prototype was rebuilt. Everything here lives in LiveConversation.processSegment(). The important structural choice is the fan-out: the on-device speaker embedding and the network transcription are kicked off together and joined with a Promise.all, so the turn costs max(embed, transcribe) rather than their sum. In practice the network branch always wins, which is why the embedder is allowed to be a WASM model on the main-thread-adjacent worker rather than something exotic.

What gets thrown away

Two guards sit around this loop and both drop work on purpose. MAX_IN_FLIGHT = 1 means only one segment is ever being processed; if James's visitor talks over the pipeline, the newer segment is dropped with a warning rather than queued — stacked Float32 buffers were OOM-killing the iPad tab. And each new turn calls abort() on the previous suggestion request, so a slow generation for turn t can never paint over the cards for turn t+1.

03Speaker ID: likelihood × prior

The prototype scored voices with mean-MFCC and a raw cosine threshold, and it was the single most broken thing in the app. The rebuild treats identification as a posterior, not a distance: 

posterior(person) ∝ likelihood(voice | person) × prior(person | place, event, recent speakers)

The likelihood is a softmax over cosine similarities against each enrolled person's centroid, at temperature 0.10. The prior is a product of independent context signals. A synthetic unknown candidate always sits in the list holding reserved mass, so a room full of strangers doesn't get force-fitted onto whoever happens to be enrolled.

Three corrections that fire outside the maths

  • One enrolled person is a special case. A softmax over a single value always returns 1.0, which would confirm every utterance as that person. The matcher bypasses softmax entirely below two candidates and falls back to a plain cosine threshold of 0.60.
  • “New person” suppresses the next match. When James knows a stranger is about to speak, the next segment is labelled Unknown regardless of score — no forced fit onto an existing cluster.
  • “Who am I speaking with?” holds attribution for 20 s. Scoped to the cluster that was just guessed, so in a three-way room the answer doesn't get glued to the wrong person. The text still reaches the transcript; only the name is withheld.

Every confirmed correction folds straight back into that person's centroid, and every segment's embedding is written to segmentEmbeddings whether it was attributed or not — which is what makes §08 possible.

04How conversation gets compacted

There is no summarisation step in the live loop, and no growing context window. Parley never sends a conversation to the model — it sends a window. The live transcript is a ten-entry ring buffer: push on the end, shift off the front, and the eleventh turn silently evicts the first. Everything older than that survives only in a compressed form that some other, slower process produced.

That gives the system several different horizons on the same conversation, each one sized for its consumer's latency budget. Nothing reads the raw whole thing except the post-conversation jobs, which can afford to.

Each of those pre-compressed artefacts has its own reduction rule, and each lands in a specific slot of the prompt. Consequence worth knowing

Because the live window is a hard ten turns with no rolling summary, a long conversation genuinely forgets its own opening. If somebody says “as I mentioned earlier” twenty turns in, the model has no access to what that was — unless the earlier fact happened to be captured as a memory during a previous conversation. That is a deliberate latency trade, not an oversight, and it is the obvious place to add a rolling mid-conversation summary later.

05What is passed to the LLM

Exactly two messages, every turn. A long system message that does not change for the life of the conversation, and a short user message that is rebuilt from scratch each time. That split is not stylistic — it is what makes prompt caching work. The system block is marked cache_control: ephemeral by the proxy, so after the first turn of a conversation the persona, style fingerprint, ban list and output contract are all cache reads.

What is deliberately absent

No audio, no embeddings, no posterior scores, no person IDs, no place IDs. The model sees names and text only. Speaker identity is settled before the prompt is built and arrives as a plain string like Mum: — the LLM is never asked to guess who spoke during a live turn. The one exception is the offline tie-breaker in §08, which is a different call entirely.

06From tokens to six tappable cards

The response is streamed, but it is JSON — so it can't simply be appended to the screen. A small scanner walks the accumulating text and emits a draft the moment a { text, category, why } object closes, which is what lets the first card land around half a second before the completion finishes. When the stream ends, the whole accumulated body is re-parsed properly; the canonical parse wins if it found at least as many suggestions as the scanner did, otherwise the scanner's output is kept rather than throwing away a partially good stream.

The filter is a belt-and-braces pair. The system prompt already asks the model not to produce banned phrases, but a phrase becomes “dead” precisely because it is a high-probability continuation, so models regenerate them anyway. If dropping leaves fewer than six cards, the grid is topped up from a small fixed set — Give me a moment, Yes, No, Could you say that again? — which are intentionally bland. They are a floor, not a feature.

07Post-conversation: the job chain

Tapping Stop does not fire off a Promise.all of enrichment work. That was the prototype's approach and it lost everything the moment the tab was torn down — which is exactly what happens when somebody closes Parley right after a conversation ends. Instead, Stop writes job descriptors into IndexedDB synchronously, and a separate drainer picks them up: immediately if the cockpit route is still mounted, otherwise on the next app open.

Every job is idempotent, so the drainer can retry freely — three attempts with exponential backoff, then the row is marked failed and left for inspection.

One more job hangs off the summariser: after it finishes, style distillation runs with force: false, which is a no-op if it last ran within twelve hours. That keeps the style fingerprint reasonably fresh without spending a smart-model call at the end of every short exchange.

08Rebuilding speaker labels with hindsight

During a conversation the matcher has to decide who is speaking with only the turns it has already heard. Afterwards there is no such constraint: every segment's embedding was saved, so the whole conversation can be clustered at once. Online seeds, offline corrects.

The pass runs in four stages, and the interesting part is the fourth guard rail — the LLM is only ever allowed to rescue segments the geometry left unresolved. It can never second-guess a confident match, because a confidently wrong model answer would silently overwrite a good label, and bad labels poison the voiceprints downstream.

09Rebuilding voiceprints

A person's voiceprint is a single 512-dimensional centroid, and it is never edited in place — it is recomputed from scratch every time, from an append-only table of contributions. Enrollment samples recorded in James's actual room, segments he confirmed or re-assigned by hand during a conversation, and segments the re-diarization pass attributed offline all land in the same table, tagged by source.

The confidence number is the mean cosine between every contribution and the resulting centroid, clamped to 0–1. It is a health signal rather than an input: a person whose samples scatter widely gets a low score, which is the cue to re-enrol them rather than to trust the matcher.

Where new people come from

Introduction detection runs in the same chain. A regex pre-filter pulls candidate names out of the transcript — “my name's…”, “call me…”, “this is…” — and a single LLM call separates genuine self-introductions from accidents like “meet me at the cafe”. Confirmed names become Person rows with status: "auto", seeded with a voiceprint built from that same conversation's embeddings, and wait in Settings → People for James or a helper to approve them. Nothing enters the roster silently.

10The context flywheel

Put §04 and §07 next to each other and the design reads as one loop. A conversation produces raw material that is far too large to prompt with; the drainer turns it into small, typed artefacts; and the next conversation's prompt is assembled out of those artefacts. The model never sees history — it sees the compressed residue of history.

11When things break

James is non-verbal. An app that shows a spinner when a provider is having a bad afternoon has taken away his voice, so every remote dependency has something below it. The ladder bottoms out in eight phrases pre-synthesised in his own cloned voice and stored as blobs in IndexedDB, which play through a bare <audio> element with no conversation object, no worker and no fetch.
